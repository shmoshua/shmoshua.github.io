What the paper shows and why it matters, in a sentence or two. Math like $\nu \ll \pi$ works.

The steps can be broken down into the following steps.
  1. Glauber satisfies WPI for every temperature step.
  2. Given WPI, Glauber mixes fast enough to the "warm start" state of the next schedule step.
  3. Each temperature step is indeed a warm start.

The Annealing algorithm

Let $\beta_0$ be the final temperature and $T$ be the time Glauber spends in each temperature. Further, $\delta$ is the temperature step. Then, the annealing schedule is defined as: $$ \beta(t):=\delta \left\lfloor \frac{t}{T}\right\rfloor,\qquad 0\le t < T(\beta_0 / \delta+1) $$ Hence, we run Glauber with stationary distribution $\mu_{\beta(t)}$ at time $t$.

Weak Poincare Inequality

This introduces the WPI and shows the Step 2 from above.

Definition (WPI)

$P$ satisfies $(\rho,\eps)$-WPI if for any function $f:\Omega \to \R$: $$ \rho\cdot \Var_\mu(f)\le \dirchform{P}{f}{ f} + \eps \cdot \text{osc}(f)^2 $$

The first idea is to use the WPI to get: $$ \frac{d}{dt}\text{Var}_{\mu}(f_t)\le -2 \dirchform{P}{f_t}{f_t}\le -2\rho\Var_\mu(f_t)+2\eps \cdot \text{osc}(f_t)^2 $$ Then, by Grönwall, we have: $$ \frac{d}{dt}\left[e^{2\rho t}\Var_\mu (f_t)\right]=2\rho e^{2\rho t}\Var_\mu(f_t)+e^{2\rho t}\frac{d}{dt}\Var_\mu(f_t)\le 2\eps e^{2\rho t}\text{osc}(f_t)^2 $$ Integrating, we have that: $$ \begin{aligned} \Var_\mu(f_T) &\le e^{-2\rho T} \Var_\mu(f_0)+2\eps \int_0^T e^{2\rho (t-T)}\text{osc}(f_t)^2dt\\ &\le e^{-2\rho T} \Var_\mu(f_0)+\frac{\eps}{\rho}\cdot \text{osc}(f_0)^2 \end{aligned} $$ Similarly, $\chi^2(\nu_t\|\mu)\le e^{-2\rho t}\chi^2(\nu_0\|\mu)+\frac{\eps}{\rho}\text{osc}(f_0)^2$.

We also additionally define warm start, if $\chi^2(\nu_0\|\mu)=O(1)$ and $\text{osc}(f_0)=O(1)$.

Glauber Mixes

Let $\mu_k:=\mu_{k\delta}$, $P_k$ the kernel with stationary distribution $\mu_k$ for time $T$. Further, let$\nu_k:=P_k\nu_{k-1}$. Then, for any $k$: $$ \begin{aligned} \tv(\nu_k,\mu_k) &\le \tv(P_k\nu_{k-1}, P_k\mu_{k-1})+ \tv(P_k\mu_{k-1}, \mu_{k}) \\ &\le \tv(\nu_{k-1}, \mu_{k-1}) + \tv(P_k\mu_{k-1}, \mu_{k}) \end{aligned} $$ If we start from uniform distribution as $\nu_0$, we have that $\tv(\nu_{0}, \mu_{0})=0$. Hence, $$ \tv(\nu_{\text{out}},\mu_{\beta_0}) \le \sum_{k = 1}^{\beta_0 / \delta} \tv(P_k \mu_{k-1},\mu_k)\le \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k = 1}^{\beta_0 / \delta} \sqrt{\chi^2( P_k\mu_{k-1}\|\mu_k)} $$ Now we just have to control the $\chi^2$-distance. From Part 1, we have that: $$ \chi^2( P_k \mu_{k-1}\|\mu_{k})\le e^{-2\rho T}\chi^2(\mu_{k-1}\|\mu_k)+\frac{\eps}{\rho}\text{osc}\left(\frac{d\mu_{k-1}}{d\mu_k}\right)^2 $$ Let $f_k:= d\mu_{k-1} / d\mu_k=\frac{\exp(-\delta H)}{\E_{\mu_k}\exp(-\delta H)}$. Let $\Delta:= \delta \|H\|_\infty$. Then, $e^{-2\Delta}\le f_k\le e^{2\Delta}$. We then have that: $$ e^{2\Delta}-e^{-2\Delta}\le e^{2\Delta} + (e^{2\Delta}-2)=2(e^{2\Delta}-1) $$ Therefore, $\text{osc}(f_k)^2 \le 4(e^{2\Delta}-1)^2$ and: $$ \chi^2(\mu_{k-1}\|\mu_{k})=\E_{\mu_k}[(f_k-1)^2]\le (e^{2\Delta}-1)^2 $$ Plugging everything back into (8), we have: $$ \chi^2(P_k\mu_{k-1}\|\mu_k)\le \left(e^{-2\rho T}+\frac{4\eps}{\rho}\right)(e^{2\Delta}-1)^2 $$ Now, we can plug everything back into (7) and get: $$ \begin{aligned} \tv(\nu_{\text{out}},\mu_{\beta_0})&\le \frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta_0}{\delta}(e^{2\Delta}-1)\left(e^{-\rho T}+2\sqrt{\frac{\eps}{\rho}}\right)\\ &\le \beta_0\|H\|_\infty e^{2\Delta}\left(e^{-\rho T}+2\sqrt{\frac{\eps}{\rho}}\right) \end{aligned} $$ where in the first inequality we use $\sqrt{a+b}\le \sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$ and in the second, $e^x-1\le xe^x$.
