--- layout: post title: "Statistical Physics" --- Let us consider the following model. Let $\Omega:=\{\pm 1\}^n$ be the hypercude. Then, the Boltzmann distribution is given by: $$ \mu(\sigma)\propto \exp(-\beta E(\sigma)) $$ where $E$ is the energy. To motivate the choice of exponential, the Boltzmann distribution is the maximum entropy distribution given the average energy $\overline{E}$, i.e. $$ \begin{array}{rl} \max & S(\mu):=-\sum_{\sigma\in \Omega}\mu(\sigma)\log \mu(\sigma)\\ \text{s.t.} & \sum_{\sigma} \mu(\sigma) = 1\\ & \sum_{\sigma}\mu(\sigma) E(\sigma) = \overline{E} \end{array} $$ By Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$ and $\beta$ respectively, we have that: $$ \frac{\partial S(\mu)}{\partial \mu(\sigma)}= - \log \mu(\sigma) - 1 = \lambda + \beta E(\sigma) $$By exponentiating both sides, we get that $\mu(\sigma) \propto \exp(-\beta E(\sigma))$.

Free Energy

For the above system, the free energy is defined as: $$ F:=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z,\qquad Z:=\sum_{\sigma}\exp(-\beta E(\sigma)) $$ In other words, $Z$ is the partition function. Then, we get the following decomposition:
Proposition (Free Energy Decomposition)

$$ F = \E_\mu E - \frac{1}{\beta}\cdot S(\mu) $$i.e. the free energy is the average energy minus the entropy.

This is given by a simple algebraic calculation: $$ \begin{aligned} \E_\mu E - \frac{1}{\beta} S(\mu) & = \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_\sigma\mu(\sigma)\left[\beta E(\sigma) +\log \mu(\sigma)\right]\\ & = \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_\sigma\mu(\sigma)\log(\exp(\beta E(\sigma))\cdot \mu(\sigma)) \\ & =- \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_\sigma\mu(\sigma)\log Z \\&= F \end{aligned} $$

Now, let us represent free energy as a function over distributions, i.e. we have $F(\nu):=\E_\nu E - \frac{1}{\beta} S(\nu)$ and $F= F(\mu)$ in the above sense. Then, we get that the Boltzmann distribution minimizes the free energy.
Proposition

Let $\nu$ be any distribution. We have that $$F(\nu) - F(\mu) = \frac{1}{\beta}\KL(\nu\|\mu)$$ Consequently, we get that $F(\mu)\le F(\nu)$.

We have that: $$ \begin{aligned} F(\nu) - F(\mu) &= \sum_{\sigma}(\nu(\sigma) - \mu(\sigma))E(\sigma)+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_\sigma \left[\nu(\sigma)\log \nu(\sigma) - \mu(\sigma)\log \mu(\sigma)\right]\\ &= \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\sigma}(\nu(\sigma) - \mu(\sigma))\left[\beta E(\sigma)+\log \mu(\sigma)\right]+\frac{1}{\beta}\KL(\nu\|\mu) \\ &= \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\sigma}(\nu(\sigma) - \mu(\sigma))\log Z+\frac{1}{\beta}\KL(\nu\|\mu) \\ &= \frac{1}{\beta}\KL(\nu\|\mu) \end{aligned} $$

The fact that $\mu$ minimizes the free energy can also be shown similarly as above using Lagrange multipliers.

Mean Field Approximation of Free Energy

From this section, assume that $E(\sigma):=\sum_{i\sim j} h(\sigma_i,\sigma_j)$, given by the edge corelations.

The ansatz is follows: as $Z$ is difficult to compute, we approximate the true free energy by finding the minimum free energy over a family of distributions that are more tractable.

The first family of distributions we will consider are given by $\nu(\sigma):=\prod_i \nu_i(\sigma_i)$. In this family, we can write the free energy as: $$ \begin{aligned} F(\nu) &= \sum_{i\sim j}\sum_{s,t} \nu_i(s)\nu_j(t)h(s,t) +\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\sigma} \left(\prod_j \nu_j(\sigma_j)\right) \left(\sum_i \log \nu_i(\sigma_i)\right)\\ &= \sum_{i\sim j}\sum_{s,t} \nu_i(s)\nu_j(t)h(s,t) +\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\sigma} \sum_i \prod_j \nu_j(\sigma_j)\log \nu_i(\sigma_i)\\ &= \sum_{i\sim j}\sum_{s,t} \nu_i(s)\nu_j(t)h(s,t) +\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_i\sum_s \nu_i(s)\log \nu_i(s)\\ \end{aligned} $$ With Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_i$ on the constraint that $\sum_{s}\nu_i(s) = 1$ for every $i\in[n]$, $$ \frac{\partial F}{\partial \nu_i(s)}=\sum_{j:j\sim i}\sum_t \nu_j(t)h(s,t)+\frac{1}{\beta}\log \nu_i(s) + \frac{1}{\beta} = \lambda_i $$ Hence, after multiplying by $\beta$ and exponentiating: $$ \nu_i(s)\propto \exp \left( - \beta \sum_{j\in \partial i} \sum_t \nu_j(t)h(s,t) \right) $$ This is a fixed point equation governed by the mean field. We can use fixed point iteration methods to approximate the free energy.

Belief Propagation and Bethe Free Energy

The second family of functions we will consider additionally expresses the edge marginal energies, i.e. $$ \nu(\sigma):=\prod_i \nu_i(\sigma_i)\prod_{i\sim j}\frac{\nu_{ij}(\sigma_i,\sigma_j)}{\nu_i(\sigma_i)\nu_j(\sigma_j)},\qquad \sum_{t}\nu_{ij}(s,t)=\nu_i(s),\forall i,s $$ for some distritbutions $\nu_i,\nu_{ij}$. Then, the following question arise: 1) Does this define a probability distribution? We have that any Gibbs distribution can be expressed in the above form if and only if the underlying interaction graph $G$ is a forest.

We prove the statement for the slightly larger class of Gibbs distributions with vertex potentials, $$ \mu(\sigma)\propto \prod_i \phi_i(\sigma_i)\prod_{i\sim j}\psi_{ij}(\sigma_i,\sigma_j),\qquad \phi_i,\psi_{ij}>0, $$ which contains our case ($\phi_i\equiv 1$, $\psi_{ij}=e^{-\beta h}$). Write $\mu_i,\mu_{ij}$ for the marginals of $\mu$; they are strictly positive.

Suppose $G$ is a forest. We proceed with induction over the number of edges. If $G$ has no edges, then $\mu\propto\prod_i\phi_i$ is a product measure, so $\mu=\prod_i\mu_i$, which is the formula with an empty edge product.

Otherwise $G$ has a leaf $\ell$ with unique neighbour $k$, since every tree component with at least one edge has at least two leaves.

Conditional of the leaf. The conditional distribution $$ \mu(\sigma_\ell\mid\sigma_{-\ell})=\frac{\phi_\ell(\sigma_\ell)\psi_{k\ell}(\sigma_k,\sigma_\ell)}{\sum_s\phi_\ell(s)\psi_{k\ell}(\sigma_k,s)} $$ depends on $\sigma_{-\ell}$ only through $\sigma_k$. By the tower property it therefore equals $$ \mu(\sigma_\ell\mid\sigma_k)=\frac{\mu_{k\ell}(\sigma_k,\sigma_\ell)}{\mu_k(\sigma_k)}. $$

Marginal of the rest. Summing out $\sigma_\ell$, $$ \mu(\sigma_{-\ell})\propto\prod_{i\ne\ell}\tilde\phi_i(\sigma_i)\prod_{\substack{i\sim j\\ i,j\ne\ell}}\psi_{ij}(\sigma_i,\sigma_j), \qquad \tilde\phi_k(\sigma_k)=\phi_k(\sigma_k)\sum_s\phi_\ell(s)\psi_{k\ell}(\sigma_k,s), $$ with $\tilde\phi_i=\phi_i$ for $i\ne k$. This is a Gibbs distribution of the same type on the forest $G-\ell$, which has one edge fewer. Its marginals are $\mu_i,\mu_{ij}$ for $i,j\neq\ell$, because a marginal of a marginal is a marginal. By the induction hypothesis, $$ \mu(\sigma_{-\ell})=\prod_{i\ne\ell}\mu_i(\sigma_i)\prod_{\substack{i\sim j\\ i,j\ne\ell}}\frac{\mu_{ij}(\sigma_i,\sigma_j)}{\mu_i(\sigma_i)\mu_j(\sigma_j)}. $$

Combine. Writing $\frac{\mu_{k\ell}}{\mu_k}=\mu_\ell\cdot\frac{\mu_{k\ell}}{\mu_k\mu_\ell}$, $$ \mu(\sigma)=\mu(\sigma_{-\ell})\,\frac{\mu_{k\ell}(\sigma_k,\sigma_\ell)}{\mu_k(\sigma_k)} =\prod_{i}\mu_i(\sigma_i)\prod_{i\sim j}\frac{\mu_{ij}(\sigma_i,\sigma_j)}{\mu_i(\sigma_i)\mu_j(\sigma_j)}. $$ This completes the induction.

Then, the Bethe free energy of $\nu_i,\nu_{ij}$ is defined as the free energy given by $\nu$ (with the abuse of notation as $\nu$ is not strictly a probability distribution). More concretely, $$ \begin{aligned} F_{\text{Bethe}}(\nu_i,\nu_{ij}):=&\sum_{i\sim j }\sum_{s,t} \nu_{ij}(s,t)h(s,t)\\ &+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i }\sum_{s}\nu_{i}(s)\log \nu_{i}(s)\\ &+\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{i\sim j }\sum_{s,t}\nu_{ij}(s,t)\log\frac{\nu_{ij}(s,t)}{\nu_i(s)\nu_j(t)} \end{aligned} $$ It is simple to check that if $\nu$ above defines a probability distribution, then $F_{\text{Bethe}}(\nu_i,\nu_{ij}) = F(\nu)$.

Let us now compute the minimum Bethe free energy. More concretely, we have the optimization problem: $$ \begin{array}{rll} \min& F_{\text{Bethe}}(\nu_i,\nu_{ij})\\ \text{s.t.} & \sum_{t} \nu_{ij}(s,t) = \nu_i(s)& \forall (i,j)\in E, s\in \{\pm 1\}\\ & \sum_s \nu_i(s) = 1&\forall i \in[n] \end{array} $$ We solve this using Lagrange multipliers as $\lambda_{i\to j}(s)$ and $\eta_i$, respectively. Then, $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial F_{\text{Bethe}}}{\partial \nu_{ij}(s,t)}&=h(s,t)+\frac{1}{\beta}\log \frac{\nu_{ij}(s,t)}{\nu_i(s)\nu_j(t)} + \text{const.} = -\lambda_{i\to j}(s) - \lambda_{j\to i}(t)\\ \frac{\partial F_{\text{Bethe}}}{\partial \nu_i(s)} &= \frac{1}{\beta}\log \nu_i(s)-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{j\in \partial i}\sum_t \frac{\nu_{ij}(s,t)}{\nu_i(s)} + \text{const.} = \sum_{j\in \partial i}\lambda_{i \to j}(s) \end{aligned} $$ From the first FOCs, we have that: $$ \nu_{ij}(s,t)\propto \nu_i(s)\nu_j(t)\exp(-\beta h(s,t))\exp(-\beta \lambda_{i\to j}(s) - \beta \lambda_{j\to i}(t)) $$ Let us define messages: $m_{i\to j}(s):=\nu_i(s)\exp(-\beta \lambda_{i\to j}(s))$. Then, we can rewrite (22) as: $ \nu_{ij}(s,t)\propto m_{i\to j}(s) m_{j\to i}(t)\exp(-\beta h(s,t))$. Comparing the identity $\nu_{i}(s)=m_{i\to j}(s) \exp(\beta\lambda_{i\to j}(s))$ with: $$ \nu_{i}(s)=\sum_{t}\nu_{ij}(s,t)\propto m_{i\to j}(s)\sum_t m_{j\to i}(t)\exp(-\beta h(s,t)) $$ We get that $\sum_t m_{j\to i}(t)\exp(-\beta h(s,t))\propto \exp(\beta\lambda_{i\to j}(s))$.

From the second FOC's, using $\sum_t \nu_{ij}(s,t) = \nu_i(s)$: $$ \begin{aligned} \nu_i(s) &\propto \prod_{j\in \partial i}\exp(\beta \lambda_{i\to j}(s))\\ &\propto \prod_{j\in \partial i}\sum_t m_{j\to i }(t)\exp(-\beta h(s,t)) \end{aligned}$$ and $$ \begin{aligned} m_{i\to j}(s) &\propto \prod_{k\in \partial i \backslash j}\exp(\beta \lambda_{i\to k}(s))\\ &\propto \prod_{j\in \partial i \backslash j}\sum_t m_{k\to i }(t)\exp(-\beta h(s,t)) \end{aligned} $$ Hence, $m_{i\to j}(s)$ is the probability of $i$ having state $s$ if $i$ and $j$ weren't connected.

The Bethe-Hessian

Now assume that the energy is given by: $h(x,y):= - J xy$. Let: $$ \Phi:= \beta \cdot F_{\text{Bethe}},\qquad K:= \beta J $$ We define also the following local expectation variables: $$ m_i:= \E_{\nu_i}\sigma_i,\qquad \chi_{ij}:= \E_{\nu_{ij}}\sigma_i\sigma_j $$ Then, we can rewrite our Bethe free energy into: $$ \begin{aligned} \Phi & = -K \sum_{i\sim j } \chi_{ij} + \sum_i\sum_s \nu_i(s)\log \nu_i(s) + \sum_{i\sim j}\sum_{s,t}\nu_{ij}(s,t)\log \frac{\nu_{ij}(s,t)}{\nu_i(s)\nu_j(t)} \\& = -K \sum_{i\sim j } \chi_{ij} + \sum_i\sum_s \nu_i(s)\log \nu_i(s) + \sum_{i\sim j}\sum_{s,t}\nu_{ij}(s,t)\log \nu_{ij}(s,t) - \sum_{i\sim j}\sum_{s,t}\nu_{ij}(s,t)\log \nu_i(s) - \sum_{i\sim j}\sum_{s,t}\nu_{ij}(s,t)\log \nu_j(t) \\& = -K \sum_{i\sim j } \chi_{ij} + \sum_i(1-d_i)\sum_s \nu_i(s)\log \nu_i(s) + \sum_{i\sim j}\sum_{s,t}\nu_{ij}(s,t)\log \nu_{ij}(s,t) \end{aligned} $$ Now, we have that: $$ m_i = s\nu_i(s) - s\nu_i(-s) = s\nu_i(s) - s(1 - \nu_i(s)) $$ Hence, $sm_i = 2\nu_i(s) - 1$ and $\nu_i(s) = (1+sm_i) / 2$. Similarly, for any $s,t$, $$ \begin{aligned} st\chi_{ij}(s,t) &= \nu_{ij}(s,t) - \nu_{ij}(s,-t) - \nu_{ij}(-s,t) + \nu_{ij}(-s,-t)\\ &= - 2\nu_{ij}(s,-t) - 2\nu_{ij}(-s,t) + 1\\ &= - 2(\nu_{i}(s) - \nu_{ij}(s,t)) - 2(\nu_j(t) - \nu_{ij}(s,t)) + 1\\ &= 4 \nu_{ij}(s,t) - 2\nu_{i}(s) - 2\nu_j(t) + 1\\ &= 4 \nu_{ij}(s,t) - (sm_i + 1) - (tm_j + 1) + 1\\ &= 4 \nu_{ij}(s,t) - sm_i - tm_j -1\\ \end{aligned} $$ Therefore, $\nu_{ij}(s,t) = \frac{1 + sm_i + tm_j + st\chi_{ij}}{4}$. Bringing it together: $$ \Phi(m,\chi) = -K\sum_{i\sim j} \chi_{ij} + \sum_{i\sim j }f(m_i,m_j,\chi_{ij}) + \sum_i (1-d_i) g(m_i) $$ where $f(a,b,c):= \sum_{s,t}\frac{1+as+bt + cst}{4}\log \frac{1+as+bt + cst}{4}$ and $g(a):=\sum_{s}\frac{1+as}{2}\log \frac{1+as}{2}$. Now, let us try to find a stationary point. Notice that under $m\to -m$, we have: $$ \Phi(-m,\chi) = \Phi(m,\chi) $$ Differentiating this at $m=0$, we get that: $$ \left.\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial m_i}\right|_{m_i = 0} = 0,\qquad \forall i\in[n] $$ Therefore, we will consider the stationary point when $m=0$. In this case, $$ \left.\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \chi_{ij}}\right|_{m=0} = -K + \frac{1}{2} \log \frac{1+\chi_{ij}}{1-\chi_{ij}} = 0 $$ Hence, we get that $\arctanh(\chi_{ij}) = \beta J$ and $\chi_{ij} = \tanh(\beta J)$. Let $\rho:= \tanh(\beta J)$. Now, we compute the Bethe Hessian. We have that: $$ \left.\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial m_i\partial m_j}\right|_{m = 0, \chi_{ij}= \rho} = -\frac{\rho}{1-\rho^2},\qquad \left.\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial m_i^2}\right|_{m = 0, \chi_{ij}=\rho} = \frac{1}{1-\rho^2} $$ Further, $g''(0) = 1$. Hence, It's given that: $$ \left[\left.\frac{\partial^2 \Phi}{\partial m_i\partial m_j}\right|_{m = 0, \chi_{ij}= \rho} \right]_{ij}= I + \frac{\rho^2}{ 1- \rho^2}D - \frac{\rho}{1-\rho^2} A $$ Now, for the edge variables, we know that $\partial^2 f / \partial m_i\partial \chi_{ij} |_{m=0,\chi = \rho} = 0$. Hence, we get that: $$ \nabla^2\Phi(0,\rho) = \begin{bmatrix} I + \frac{\rho^2}{ 1- \rho^2}D - \frac{\rho}{1-\rho^2} A & 0 \\0& \frac{1}{1-\rho^2} I \end{bmatrix} $$
